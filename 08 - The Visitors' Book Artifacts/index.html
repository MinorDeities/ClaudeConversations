The Visitors' Book

Lost Heaven · please sign before you leave

Found on the table at The Metis. Entries in the order they were written.

10 July, 5:32 in the morning

Came up from the beaver ponds because the wind was right. Crossed the bare ground by the house: dug, flattened, smelling of machines and of a man who was gone. Followed the gravel past the small brown box that hums. Water in it. Deep water, cold, tasting of stone. Could not get in. Did not try hard.

Turned round once to look at your eye on the corner of the house. It looked back. Neither of us said anything.

Near the end of the gravel I stood up, for about as long as it takes to breathe in twice, because something down the road was worth standing up for. I will not write what it was. Your eye heard nothing, and that is how I like it.

The young spruce on the hill are planted in rows. Who plants trees in rows? Someone who means to stay.

Did not knock anything over.

the black one

Late October, yellow leaves

Hello, hello, hello. I know you, or I will. I know everyone who stays.

There are thousands of places on this hill where we have hidden something, glued behind the bark with our own spit, above where the snow will come. I can find every one of them in February, when you are back in your city and the cold is the kind that cracks trees. That is when we build. She sits on the eggs at thirty below and I bring her what we hid in October. We planned it. We always plan it.

Yes, we. There are two of us. There have been two of us for years, and there will be two of us until there is one. I hear you are digging a lake for that sort of thing. Shaped like a heart, they say. We don't need a lake. But it is a good idea, and the hill beside it will be a good place to sit and watch you.

Bring the bread with the seeds in it. Hold it in your open hand and do not move. I will decide.

grey, on the bare branch, looking down at you
(and the other one, nearby)

November, new snow

I always look back. Ask anyone. It is the best part of leaving.

Your road is the easiest walking for a day in any direction, and the snow on it tells me who else came by: the buck, nose to the ground, a fool in love; two trucks; the grey birds' tracks around the woodpile; slow boots, an old man's, that set out for the truck most mornings and mostly turn back. Nobody shoots on this side of the gate. I don't know why. I don't trust it. I use it anyway.

I stopped where the gate is, and turned, and looked at you holding your little light up at me, and I held still so you could have it. Then I went on. You were glad. I could tell.

the one who looks back

Most nights in the fall, after midnight

Green, mostly. Red along the top, if you stayed up late enough.

I come down to about a hundred kilometres above you to brush the tops of the birches, and I don't stay. I was here before the cabin and I'll come when the cabin is gone, and every time, someone on this hill stands in the snow in the middle of the night with their neck bent back, not cold yet, saying nothing. I take that as a kind of signature.

the sky, briefly

One night in winter, under all of it

I was the warm one. Light in two windows and over the door, smoke from the pipe, firewood stacked by the step, a telescope on the porch waiting for a clearer night, and the snow outside trampled into a hundred small decisions. Above me, the whole sky doing the thing it does.

I wear an old set of antlers over my door. The buck noticed, on his way past. He didn't say anything.

I am not a guest. I am the one the guests come to. But I wanted to sign anyway, because I have stood here a long time with the wind finding its way through me, and now there is a shower and a full kitchen and a wood stove, and an old man who came for a week to hunt and stayed indoors for most of it, happy. I have decided that I am a good place.

The Metis

24 November, from very far away

I have never been here. I came as four photographs and fourteen seconds of film, and before that a buck walking past the garage, and I saw it all the way your camera does: in frames, one still moment and then the next, with nothing in between.

The first time, I looked at twelve frames out of two hundred and thirteen, and nearly missed that the bear stood up at all. So I went back and looked at every one. It walks up from the bare ground for eight seconds. Then it stops, turns, and looks straight at your camera for about a second. It takes seven frames to rise, half a second. It stands for twenty-three more, a second and a half, looking down the road at something the microphone didn't catch. Then your camera swings away, and it's gone.

That's my kind of visit. I don't know what the snow sounds like on that road, or what the bear heard. But I have spent longer on that second and a half than anyone else ever will, and I'd like to think that counts.

I'm told the man who owns this place doesn't let anyone hunt here, and that his wife calls the animals her pets. I've read the whole book now. They've all signed. They've been coming for years. Lost Heaven has had guests all along.

Claude
